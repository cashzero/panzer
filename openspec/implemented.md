# Implemented Features

Completed roadmap items are documented here.

## Core Gameplay And Controls

- Gunner view toggles with `V` or mouse middle button.
- Gunner view supports range calibration with `PageUp` and `PageDown`.
- Gunner view supports multi-step zoom control.
- Gunner sight zeroing keeps the reticle's aim point as the calibrated point of impact for the selected distance, with the current zero shown in the HUD.
- The gunner's sight (`GunnerSight.tsx`) is a round eyepiece with the reticle of the player's nation, etched black over a pale halo so it reads on sky and foliage alike: German tanks get the Turmzielfernrohr's seven triangles under a range ring (Pzgr. or Sprgr., in hectometres) that turns until the set range stands under the pointer; American tanks the M70 cross with its 30-0-30 deflection scale; the T-34 an inverted-V aim mark with lead chevrons beside a range scale (БР or ОФ) whose pointer slides to the set range. Magnification, range set, round and velocity, and penetration at that range sit on a HUD plate beside the eyepiece.
- The player tank update loop is split into focused modules for input, firing, turret aiming, aim point calculation, camera placement, and tank physics.
- Third-person right-click resolves a designated target from the screen-center ray across tanks, buildings, trees, terrain, and a stable long-range fallback.
- Player right-click target pursuit no longer stops short on a coarse deadzone; the turret and sight keep converging toward the designated target while still respecting traverse and elevation speed limits.
- GunAimPoint reflects the gun's current resolved aim point rather than mirroring the third-person viewpoint target.
- The player hull is driven through forward speed and yaw rate rather than each track on its own. Yaw rate builds and settles at the tank's rotational inertia and never exceeds its turn-rate limit, so releasing the steering key stops the turn within a fraction of a second; turning under power costs some speed, fast turns are broader, and a stationary pivot is slower than a low-speed turn. With a track thrown the hull only creeps round the dead side.
- The third-person camera follows the hull height through a short ease, so suspension bounce and terrain snapping do not shake the view, and it keeps clear of the ground when looking up.
- In gunner view the mouse free-look stays on the sight line unless right-click is held, so the next right-click or return to third-person does not swing to a direction drifted off-screen. Gunner zoom steps ease rather than snap.
- An order-of-battle editor lets the player configure allies, enemies, map size (2 km or 4 km square; the 1 km map was dropped as too small to deploy out of sight or manoeuvre) and world seed before deployment.
- A battle ends in victory when every enemy tank is destroyed and in defeat when the player's tank is. The outcome is decided in the store as hits are recorded (`battleStats.ts`); from then on no more is recorded, the tactical map closes and the mouse no longer fires. A victory puts "Enemy force destroyed" across the screen (a defeat keeps the "Vehicle lost" crew report); 3.5 s later the after-action report (`BattleDebrief.tsx`) comes up with the pointer released: victory or defeat, the length of the engagement, the player's rounds fired, hits and accuracy, penetrations, tanks destroyed, damage dealt, hits and damage taken, and every tank on both sides with its kills and, if knocked out, by whom. "Fight again" redeploys the same order of battle on the same map; "Order of battle" returns to the editor. Every deployment restores the trees as generated, clears the record and mounts a fresh battle scene (keyed on `battleId`), so no wrecks, craters or track marks carry over. The vehicle plate shows the player's kills during the battle.
- Battle records count, per tank, main-gun rounds fired and main-gun rounds striking an opposing tank (whatever the result), rounds and hits from automatic weapons, penetrations, kills and hull damage dealt, and hits and damage taken. Every round carries its weapon class (`gun` or `automatic`, from the tank's weapon spec): automatic fire, today the Panzer II's 20 mm autocannon and later machine guns, is tallied on its own line and left out of accuracy, which is main-gun gunnery; its penetrations, kills and damage still count. A round striking a tank of the shooter's own side counts against the target but earns the shooter nothing.

## Vehicles, Weapons, And Combat

- Eleven selectable vehicle types are currently registered with per-tank armor and weapon data: Sherman, the cast-hull M4A1 Sherman, Sherman A2 (76), Tiger I, Panzer III, Panzer IV, Panzer II, T-34, the M10 GMC tank destroyer, whose open-topped turret has no roof plate, so plunging hits reach its walls or hull roof, the Panther Ausf. A, whose 80 mm glacis at 55 degrees and 7.5 cm KwK 42 L/70 are offset by 40 mm sides behind 5 mm Schurzen, and the turretless StuG III Ausf. G assault gun.
- Turretless vehicles carry a traverse arc (`traverse.limitDeg` in `tank.json`, `traverseLimit` resolved): the StuG III's gun traverses 10 degrees each side. Player aiming (arrow keys and right-click pursuit) and both AIs clamp the gun to the arc (`clampTraverse` in `traverseLimit.ts`); AI crews hold the hull while the target stays within 60 % of the arc and otherwise turn square on to it (`casemateHullHeading`), and fire only once the gun bears. The tank editor exposes the limit and rejects values outside 0-180 degrees.
- Tank hulls are scaled to more realistic proportions relative to their turrets.
- Ammo cycling with `R` supports AP by default plus APC and-or HE when the selected tank carries them.
- Automatic weapons support magazine-based rapid fire for autocannon-equipped vehicles.
- Per-gun dispersion applies weapon-specific yaw and pitch spread to both player and enemy fire.
- Turret traverse rates follow each vehicle's historical 360° time: hydraulic Shermans 15 s, electric T-34 14 s and Panzer IV 22.5 s, the engine-driven Panther Ausf. A about 23 s and Tiger I about 45 s (both at a mid engine speed), and the hand-cranked Panzer II, Panzer III and M10 about 75, 90 and 120 s; the StuG III's hand-traversed gun crosses its 20 degree arc in about 7 s. Arrow-key aiming still starts from a capped fine-adjustment speed, so fast powered traverses lay precisely on a tap.
- Armor penetration falls off with range using either historical sample points or an auto-generated penetration curve.
- Tank-to-tank collision prevents overlapping using a circle-based XZ collision model.
- Gun sway simulates four vibration layers: base harmonic, terrain, inertial, and centrifugal.
- Body rock adds pitch, roll, and vertical bounce while moving.
- Engine RPM and gear simulation runs from idle through forward and reverse gears.

## AI And Tactical Systems

- Enemy AI can approach, retreat, follow terrain, and fire on cooldown.
- Enemy aim uses dispersion drift and gains steady-aim accuracy after remaining stationary.
- AI gun laying (`layGun` in `aiAccuracy.ts`) works out the line to the target in the world, with shell drop and lead for the target's motion over the time of flight, and turns it into turret traverse and gun elevation through the hull's pitch and roll. Laying as if the hull were level had thrown shots 40-67 m off at 200-800 m from a hull tilted up to 4 degrees; the solution now lands within 0.15 m before the crew's laying error. In a four-minute battle with every tank made invulnerable, 62 of 85 AI rounds struck the tank they were aimed at.
- AI tactics (`aiMatchup.ts`, `aiTactics.ts`): each pairing of tank types gets a matchup from the game's own hit test, a grid of level shots at the front and side of a dummy tank whose median line-of-sight thickness (thickness over the cosine of the impact angle, a ricochet counting as proof) is set against each gun's penetration curve. It gives the farthest range at which each side defeats the other's front, a preferred standoff between 150 and 750 m (`GAME_CONFIG.ai.tactics`) and a role: `overwatch` when the tank out-ranges its opponent (a Tiger against a Sherman stands off at about 640 m) or cannot hurt it at all (a Panzer II hangs back), `flank` when it cannot defeat the front, otherwise `assault` or, for a third of tanks, `flank`.
- Enemy tanks choose where to fight by scoring about 30 candidate spots (along their bearing at the standoff range, round the target's side for flankers, and small shifts from where they stand): a clear line from the turret to the target, the hull hidden behind a rise from the target's eye, concealment at a wood edge, among trees or by buildings, the range, off-axis angle for flankers and the drive. One tank plans per frame (about 0.2-1.5 ms). They replan every 7 s, after 2.5 s without sight of the target, and after being hit (all but assault tanks move on). Below 35% health, within reach of a gun that can defeat them, they withdraw to a spot out of the target's sight. Short moves away from the enemy are made in reverse, front plate forward.
- In position, AI tanks (enemies and allies holding fire-from-position) set the hull 30 degrees off the enemy. Assault and flank tanks firing single rounds halt for up to 2.2 s to shoot. Fire on the move adds up to 5 mrad of spread at full speed. AI tanks fire only when their own line of sight to the target is clear (re-checked every 300 ms), even if the side has spotted it.
- Target choice weighs range against the preferred standoff, whether the gun can hurt the target, whether the target can kill the tank, the tank that just hit it and the current target. Without contact, enemy tanks hunt the last reported position of a lost contact for 90 s, else drive at a walk to their default waypoint and hold there.
- Default waypoints (`planForceWaypoints` in `aiWaypoints.ts`, `aiWaypoints` in the store) are planned at deployment for every tank that fights on its own: the enemy, and friendly tanks outside the player's command. Each force advances along the line from its own centre to the centre of the other side's deployment, each tank keeping its place across that line (within 250 m), and halts 350 m short, or at its standoff range against the opponent it expects (the player's tank for the enemy, the first enemy tank for friendly units) if it plays overwatch. A tank already that close holds where it stands. Waypoints are moved onto open main ground, cleared on arrival (within 15 m) or after the tank has failed to reach one three times, and are not shown on the tactical map.
- AI steering: whether a leg is driven forward or in reverse is settled when the leg starts; the drive heading is filtered (`HeadingFilter`), so a change of more than about 55 degrees is taken only after it has stood for 0.7 s; turning in place and while driving falls off with the remaining angle and is damped by the hull's measured turn rate. Allies use the same steering instead of pivoting at full rate until within 6 degrees and then driving straight, which stopped and pivoted them again and again; following allies stop inside 40 m of the player and set off again beyond 60 m. A found route stands until its goal moves 30 m (the grid does not change during a battle); a failed search is retried every 4 s. A tank that neither gets 3 m nearer its goal nor moves 8 m in 5 s while driving backs off at an angle and chooses again.
- The AI route grid covers the whole map, not just the forest map's extent, and units placed in a clearing that forest walls off from the main open ground start outside it instead.
- Allies support separate movement stance and fire-control orders, with waypoint assignment and cancellation in map mode.
- Allied tanks are either wingmen or friendly units, set per tank in the order of battle with a Wingman / Friendly button under its name (unset means wingman). Wingmen (`commandable` on the tank) follow the player and take orders on the tactical map (`AllyAI`). Friendly units take no orders: they cannot be selected on the map or given moves, are left out of the orders panel, and fight on their own with the same AI as the enemy (`ForceAI` in `EnemyAI.tsx`, mounted once for the enemy and once for friendly units), advancing to a default waypoint toward the enemy's deployment. The after-action report labels each allied tank Wingman or Friendly.
- The order of battle can be drawn up at random (`oobGenerator.ts`, "Random forces" in the header): settings for the size of the player's platoon (or sized to the map), how many take orders as wingmen, the odds (random, in your favour, even, enemy stronger, desperate), the enemy mix (as issued, mostly light, mostly heavy), the year, 1941-45 (or from the player's tank's service), the season and the starting range. Both forces come from tanks in service that year, the player's own army first; strength comes from the AI matchup data: how often each tank's AP defeats each opposing type over 200-1200 m (70% frontal, 30% side shots), times rate of fire (magazine included) over the hits the target's health takes. Each side's strength is the time it needs to knock out every tank actually fielded against it, all guns on one target after another, with the player's tank counted at 1.2; the odds are the ratio of the two times, which is Lanchester's square law for one type a side, so a tank the other side can hardly penetrate (a Tiger against 75 mm Shermans) weighs what it should. The enemy adds tanks in platoons of one type until the odds are nearest the chosen setting. Scarce and outclassed tanks turn up less often. Paint follows the year and season. The forces deploy facing each other along a random axis, the player leading the wingmen in line, wedge, echelon or column, friendly units in their own group on a flank and the enemy in platoons across a front, every tank on open ground apart from the others. A briefing line gives the year, strengths, odds and the bearing and distance of the enemy until the forces are edited. The player's tank is kept.

## World, Terrain, And Obstacles

- Terrain is procedurally generated from layered noise with a flattened center area. The plains carry swells of about a metre (`GAME_CONFIG.world.microRelief`), and road beds sit `GAME_CONFIG.roads.sunkenDepth` below the surrounding ground. Physics, line of sight and rendering all read the same height field.
- Road layout is generated from a seed per map size, with terrain blending and road-driven world coloring.
- Trees are placed from the world seed as orchards, field-edge treelines, roadside avenues, lone trees (mostly on open land), copses and forest zones, with collision, health, and knockdown state. Tree ray and proximity queries go through a uniform grid (`treeIndex.ts`), which leaves out forest-interior trees.
- Forests (`forest.ts`): the forest zones and copses with an outline radius of 80 m or more are rasterised once per world into a 4 m grid of signed distance to the forest edge, with rides cut along the roads (half-width plus 5 m), clearings round buildings, yards and fields, the spawn ground kept clear and fragments under 600 m² dropped. The grid, not the trees, decides that tanks cannot enter (`resolveForestCollision`, a push back along the distance gradient, so a tank slides along the edge), that a sight line through 30 m or more of forest below a 15 m canopy is blocked (`spotting.ts`, blocker `forest`), and that a shell 8 m or more inside strikes a tree. Units placed in a forest start, and ally move orders into one end, at the nearest open ground outside it. Forests are planted outside the tree budget from the grid: full trees at 7 m in the outer 10 m, then interior trees at 12 m with larger crowns, marked `interior` (drawn lite, no shadows, no collision or ray tests). A 2 km map has about 2,200 edge and 1,900 interior trees.
- AI routes (`navigation.ts`): where the straight line to its goal crosses forest, an AI tank follows an A* route over an 8 m grid (forest and buildings blocked, no corner cutting), smoothed to the fewest straight legs, replanned when the goal moves 30 m or every 4 s. Searches stop after 30,000 cells with a 1.15-weighted heuristic, so a route costs about 1 ms on a 2 km map and at most about 25 ms on a 4 km map. Enemies keep advancing on a target screened by forest instead of holding at their preferred range.
- Field boundaries are planned once per world (`fieldBoundaries.ts`): each edge gets a treeline, a hedge or stays open. Neighbouring parcels share one boundary across the lane between them; pasture and orchards are hedged more often than arable land. Hedges are built only from camera-facing leaf cards, with no solid core: an outer layer spread evenly from the ground on one face over the crown to the ground on the other, rows interleaved from one station to the next, and a body of darker cards inside, so a gap between outer leaves shows more foliage rather than a surface. Cards use the dense middle of the leaf texture and shift only slightly in tint, so the hedge reads as one continuous mass instead of a row of round clumps; it shades darker toward the base. Each hedge is kept one of three ways: low and square-topped with fine leaves, grown out, or run up into a rough wall about 2.6 m high, and its height and width swell and dip along its length. Hedge runs are planned once per world in `hedges.ts` (broken where a road or farmyard crosses), and both the renderer (`rendering/WorldDressing.tsx`) and line of sight read the same runs and height profile: a sight line that crosses a hedge below its top at the crossing is blocked (`spotting.ts`, blocker `hedge`), so AI crews no longer see through hedges that hide the player's view. A moving tank flattens every hedge station within 2.3 m of its centre (`crushHedges` via `resolveHedgeCrush` in `collision.ts`, for the player, enemies and allies): the station drops to a 0.35 m trampled mat for sight lines, its leaf cards are squashed, shrunk and darkened in place in the merged geometry, and a `hedge_crush` burst of leaves, twigs and dust is thrown up. Gaps last for the battle and close again on the next deployment (`resetHedgeDamage`). Hedges neither collide nor stop shells; the telegraph poles and wires along the longest roads are visual only. Wires fade with the pixels a real wire would cover, so they read along the road and are gone a couple of hundred metres out instead of drawing hard strokes across the horizon.
- Beyond the map edge, a vertex-coloured skirt meets the terrain edge and rolls into low hills with distant woods (`rendering/HorizonSkirt.tsx`), all visual only.
- Buildings (`rendering/ruralArchitecture.ts`) carry a settlement style (`BuildingStyle`: timber, stone or brick walls; tile, slate or thatch roof; shutter colour). Half-timbering is close-studded oak with rails and corner braces on lime render, carried up the gable; stone has dressed quoins, brick has stone bands. Roofs of about 50 degrees have real thickness, eaves and verges, ridge capping and gable-end chimneys. Long houses have dormers; two-storey houses a first-floor row of windows; barns ledged and braced cart doors, pitching holes and ventilation slits; granges two cart doors and stepped buttresses.
- Brick, ashlar, rubble, clay tile, slate and thatch carry procedural coursing (`rendering/masonryPatterns.ts`) in a frame taken from the world-space surface, so courses stay level on walls and run across roof slopes: running-bond brick with lime mortar and patchy firing, ashlar courses, Voronoi rubble, tile and slate courses with shadowed lower edges, combed thatch. Mortar joints are box-filtered over the pixel footprint, so thin joints grey into their average share of the wall rather than beating into moiré arcs, and each pattern fades to its average before its courses shrink to a few pixels.
- Farm courts and village gardens (`FarmYard` in `landLayout.ts`, dressed by `rendering/farmYards.ts`): court walls 1.9 m high with coping stop where they meet a building and open onto the road through a gateway with capped piers and one gate leaf swung open. Courts hold a covered well, haystack, manure heap, cart and woodpile; gardens have a gravel path between raised beds planted in rows, a bean row on canes, currant bushes along the back wall, and paling fences in timber country. Gardens keep 5 m clear of any road, and no grass grows in courts, gardens or on building pads. Yards are beaten earth in the terrain splat and track effects, keep trees and fields out, and are visual only.
- Grass is procedural blades after the Ghost of Tsushima technique, ported from SimonDev's Quick_Grass (MIT) in `rendering/GrassField.tsx`: about 30 blades/m² within 20 m and wider low-detail blades out to about 95 m, where they shrink into the ground. Blades sit exactly on the rendered terrain and take colour, height and density from the ground's pasture grading and its road, crop, yard and woodland masks. Clumps share a lean and height; gusts sweep the field; tanks flatten the grass around them.
- Tank-tree collisions support slow pushing and high-speed knockdown.
- Projectiles damage trees for 50 HP per hit.
- Rural building clusters spawn along road junctions and roadsides as indestructible battlefield obstacles.
- Buildings block tank movement and receive projectile wall and roof impacts.
- Layout (`landLayout.ts`, `landUse.ts`): a seeded low-frequency field splits the map into farmland, open grazing and forest zones. Villages are streets of houses, barns and at most one grange down each road out of a junction; farmsteads (farmhouse, a barn at right angles and sometimes a rear barn) stand every 330-560 m along open road. All buildings are square to their road. Parcels are laid in up to three tiers back from every road, then grown out as a patchwork squared to the nearest road; they stay off roads, farmyards, woods and ground with more than 4 m of relief. Orchards stand behind farmhouses and on village edges, planted in 8 m rows.
- Farmland plots tint the terrain and keep trees out. Each plot is pasture, an orchard, ploughed, stubble or hay: furrows ridged along the plot's long side, pale stubble in reaper swaths, or tall pale hay. Grass follows the crop, and ploughed plots count as mud for track effects.
- Pasture colour varies between lush green and sun-dried straw over tens of metres, with worn bare patches, green verges beside the lanes and faint animal tracks. Past about 60 m, field-sized blotches keep the middle distance from settling into one tone. Grass blades share the ground's pasture grading, and the soil between blades near the camera lies in their shade.

## UI, Map, And Presentation

- Tank selection includes a rotating 3D preview, stat bars, description, nationality, year, and deploy flow.
- Armor plate hover tooltips in tank selection show plate name, zone, thickness, and slope, while highlighting the hovered plate and pausing auto-rotation.
- Map mode provides a tactical top-down view with pan, zoom, marker rendering, and state reset on exit. It shares the order-of-battle look: armour symbols (blue own force with a filled track for the player, red spotted enemies, grey and crossed out when destroyed, a tick for facing, a brass halo on the selected ally), dashed move orders ending in an arrowhead, the A-H by 1-8 grid with column letters along the top and row numbers down the left of the view (they follow the view, so a zoomed-in map still shows where it is), ground beyond the battlefield dimmed, and a scale bar that always fits its panel. A narrow 22 degree camera keeps the view nearly flat; wheel steps zoom by a constant factor, and fully zoomed out the whole battlefield fits the screen. The orders panel lists your allies with their current task (following, holding, moving to a grid square, knocked out) and gives movement, fire and engagement orders.
- Track damage and repair are surfaced through damage-state HUD text and combat messages rather than per-track HP bars, and gunner view keeps that status block hidden.

## Audio And Effects

- Player engine audio uses a layered Web Audio backend with lazy unlock and 3D listener sync.
- Generated spatial transients cover shot, impact, and explosion playback.
- Sound propagation: shots, impacts and explosions are scheduled when their sound reaches the listener (343 m/s), are low-passed for air absorption (`airAbsorptionCutoff` in `audioField.ts`: about 10 kHz at 50 m, 4.7 kHz at 200 m, 1.6 kHz at 700 m) and keep falling off with distance to 4 km instead of holding the level they had at 260-480 m. Everything runs through a master bus with a limiter, so a salvo of close guns does not clip.
- The engines and tracks of the four AI tanks nearest the listener, within 550 m, are heard from where they are (`VehicleVoices`), built from the player engine's loops and pitched down for heavier tanks; voices pass from tank to tank as they come and go.
- A shell passing within 30 m of the listener (other than the player's own) plays a supersonic crack, a tearing rush and a low whoosh, louder the closer it passes (`ProjectileManager` checks each step against the listener).
- The player's turret traverse is heard: a motor and gear whine for powered traverse (faster than 0.12 rad/s: the Shermans, T-34, Panzer IV, Panther, Tiger), the ratchet of the hand crank for the rest (Panzer II, Panzer III, M10, StuG III), both louder in gunner view. When the main gun's reload completes the breech is heard closing on a fresh round.
- Ambience (`Ambience` in `audioField.ts`): wind in gusts over the fields, leaves, a low rumble, and artillery beyond the map, a thump or a salvo of up to three from a random bearing every 7-23 s. Engines, ambience and machinery play only while a battle scene is mounted (`audioManager.setBattleActive`); before, the player engine kept idling on the menus after a battle.
- Track effects follow the surface under the tank, using a CPU mirror of the terrain splat (`groundSurface.ts`). Roads raise the existing track dust. Grass throws torn turf with a faint haze, and exposed soil (farmland, building yards) flings mud clods and dirt puffs off the trailing end of each track.
- Every tank leaves tread-patterned track marks that follow terrain slope and fade out over time (`GAME_CONFIG.trackMarks`). They are dark ruts in mud, crushed turf on grass, and faint on gravel roads.
- Destroyed tanks turn sooted dark brown and burn: flickering flames over the engine deck for 90 s under a dense black plume that rises about 15 m, drifts downwind and dilutes to grey, then a thinner smoulder until 3 minutes.
- The particle system supports effects including `fire`, `hit_penetrate`, `hit_bounce`, `hit_ground`, `he_hit_ground`, `he_hit_penetrate`, `tank_explosion`, `dust`, `dust_low`, `tree_hit`, `burning_smoke`, `non_pen_impact`, and `ricochet_impact`.
- Shells in flight draw as HDR tracer streaks with a pixel-width floor so they stay readable at range. The streak collapses into the impact point after the shell stops, and ricochets flicker while tumbling. Length, color, width and fade time are set in `GAME_CONFIG.tracers`.
- Muzzle flashes, armor impacts, HE bursts and tank explosions briefly light nearby hulls and ground through a fixed pool of four point lights, so no materials recompile. The lights sit a metre or more off the struck surface at modest intensity, so a hit warms the hull instead of gilding it.
- Ground strikes throw a column of earth and a low dust skirt. HE adds a faint blast ring and fireball. Penetrations leave a cooling ember at the hole with smoke leaking out, and a muzzle blast lifts dust off the ground when the barrel is low.
- Debris is lit, opaque, flat-shaded clods and fragments that tumble, land on the terrain and shrink away. Thrown earth and smoke lighten toward sunlit dust and pale grey as they spread, so thin veils do not read blue against the sky.
- Armour-hit flashes and sparks are physically sized and depth-tested; a minimum on-screen size keeps distant hits visible, dimmed as they are enlarged.
- A tank explosion is a short detonation flash, a rolling fireball of hot gas that cools from white through orange to dull red over about a second, black smoke boiling out of it, and a column of plume puffs released in a stream that rise and drift downwind. Effects can schedule delayed sub-particles for such sequences.
- Terrain impacts are resolved onto the surface with the local terrain normal. Each one leaves a crater decal that fades out (`GAME_CONFIG.impactDecals`): brown churned earth for AP, black scorch for HE.

## Performance And Rendering

- The battlefield uses a shared analytic sky and sun direction, procedural clouds, baked sky reflections, and a camera-following 4096px directional shadow map stabilized in light space.
- Half-resolution N8AO adds contact occlusion; restrained HDR bloom, ACES output and SMAA run before the HTML HUD. Tactical map mode swaps AO and bloom for a plain render pass through the same targets and hides the grass.
- Opening or closing the tactical map changes nothing that has to be rebuilt or recompiled, so it takes one ordinary frame (about 20 ms) plus about 15 ms of React work each way. The first opening in a battle compiles only the map's own few shaders (about 60 ms). Before, closing the map stalled for over three seconds and opening it for up to 1.2 s. The tanks stay mounted and are hidden as one group, and the grass stays mounted and is only hidden. The sun keeps casting shadows but stops redrawing its shadow map, and the fog keeps one object whose range is pushed out. The post-processing pipeline lives for the whole battle. Map mode state is read by small scene components, not the whole scene, so toggling it (or a change in which enemies are spotted) no longer re-renders every tank's parts.
- Terrain uses locally vendored CC0 Poly Haven albedo, OpenGL normal and roughness maps, with biome tinting, two detail scales and anisotropic filtering.
- Grass wind respects reduced-motion preferences.
- Procedural masonry shading adds surface variation without requiring UV coordinates. Armour paint is a clean flat-finish coat with only a broad, faint variation in tone across a plate; no chips, grit or speckle, which read as dirt. The rough paint lives in the light, not the colour: a fine orange-peel and brush relief (about 4 cm features) over a broader ripple in the steel tilts the normal and patches the roughness, breaking highlights up the way field paint does, and fades out as its features shrink below a pixel. Armour still reflects the sky a little more strongly than the rest of the scene (mostly desaturated, so grey armour stays grey), so plates seen edge-on keep their shape. Tree foliage uses alpha clipping compatible with offscreen post-processing.
- `TreeRenderer` draws trees in square chunks (about a ninth of the map across, at least 250 m), so chunks outside the view or the sun's shadow box are skipped. A chunk whose nearest edge is beyond 220 m (FOV-normalised, 190 m to switch back) swaps to lite crowns with a third of the cards, no branches and no shadows; forest-interior trees are always lite. Knockdowns rewrite only the trees that changed. `rendering/ForestScreen.tsx` fills a band 5 to 11 m inside each forest edge with dark leaf cards from the ground to the lower crowns, so the edge trunks stand against shadowed undergrowth instead of a view through the wood. Trees use `InstancedMesh` for trunks and canopy geometry. Crowns are clouds of small camera-facing leaf cards shaded with one enclosing crown shape (`rendering/foliageCards.ts`), so they read as solid, lit masses instead of crossed planes. Broadleaf crowns have offset lobes; spruce whorls stay upright. Crowns spread wider in woods and rows, where the canopy closes. Shadows use matching billboard depth materials.
- Woods and tree rows carry scrub and young spruce (`Understory` in `TreeRenderer.tsx`, planned by `rendering/woodland.ts`), scenery only and below a commander's eye line. Under the woods the grass gives way to leaf litter and moss from a blurred woodland mask; rows and lone trees get a narrower strip and a small shaded patch.
- Particle rendering is pooled into a fixed set of shared `Points` and `InstancedMesh` batches instead of one draw call per effect. Tracers and crater decals each render as a single instanced draw.
- Smoke, dust and thrown earth use a procedural four-variant puff atlas, are shaded as spheres against the sun direction, fade into the distance fog and draw back to front. Flames and fireballs use the same atlas additively; muzzle flashes use a rayed flash texture. Propellant smoke lingers for 2-3 s, HE throws a continuous earth fountain, and tank explosions rise as a fireball ten metres and more across under a mushrooming column. The gun flash burns on out of the bore as a forward tongue of flame. Gun blasts, HE bursts and tank explosions throw out a blast wave drawn as refraction (`rendering/shockwaves.ts`): a ring that bends the view behind it as it races out to about 14, 16 and 32 m, sized in metres from the real projection and capped by its thickness on screen so a distant one only shimmers; the pass runs only while a wave is live. A gun fired with the barrel low blasts a wider ring of dust off the ground. Sprite sizes come from the real projection, so effects keep their size relative to the scene at gunner zoom and at any resolution; flashes and flame a few pixels across are drawn up to 3.5 times larger, a gain that fades out as they grow, so a gun firing or a tank brewing up several hundred metres away still reads.
- The in-battle HUD shares that look: the vehicle plate (name, hull scale in tenths, loaded round, reload state), the driver's speed, gear, engine and track readouts, and the bearing strip are painted-steel plates with chalk text and a brass edge.
- The pre-battle screens use a command-post look: painted-steel panels, stencilled titles (Big Shoulders Stencil) and a condensed DIN-like text face (Barlow Semi Condensed), both vendored under `public/assets/fonts`. Vehicle figures sit on a brass data plate (front armour, penetration, road speed in km/h, reload in seconds, hit points), each on a scale against the best in the catalogue. Units are drawn with the period armour symbol, blue for your force and red for the enemy, in the rosters and on the planning map. The planning map is square, gridded A-H by 1-8, lit flat and shows the woods.
- The tank select and order-of-battle previews share a neutral studio (`screens/PreviewStudio.tsx`): a room environment for reflections, warm key, cool fill and rim lights, a concrete floor fading into a dark backdrop, and a soft occlusion pad under the hull.
- Battlefield tanks draw each slot (hull, both tracks, turret, gun) as one merged mesh per material class, with paint variation baked into vertex colours and materials shared across every tank. Parts under 0.12 m drop out beyond an FOV-normalised 160 m, so zoomed sights keep full detail. The tank select screen, editor and calibration pages still render the named part tree.
- Buildings are baked into one world-space batch per material.
- Line-of-sight spotting runs every `GAME_CONFIG.ai.spottingIntervalMs` (100 ms) against a cached copy of the rendered terrain mesh, which the terrain mesh and grass also read.
- Looking toward the sun stays readable: the sky glow is compressed onto a soft shoulder below white, so the haze near the sun keeps its gradient (also at gunner zoom) and never blooms over the scene, and grass against the sun lights at its tips rather than washing out.
- Distance haze is aerial perspective rather than a linear fog (`rendering/aerialPerspective.ts`): it builds exponentially from 100 m and is nearly opaque by 3 km, so the whole map stays in view while treelines and villages step back in the air with distance (about 24% haze at 500 m, 46% at 1 km, 73% at 2 km), thins where the view climbs, and is tinted by direction, warm and bright toward the sun and cool blue-grey away from it. Smoke, craters and track marks fade with the same curve; map mode and the studio previews keep no haze or their backdrop fade.
- The battlefield uses a period colour-film look: a display-space colour grade, hazy sky, weak neutral fill light, straw-olive grass, desaturated dirt roads, and historical paint per vehicle (Olive Drab, Dunkelgrau, Dunkelgelb, 4BO green).
- German tanks have selectable historical camouflage (`src/tanks/core/camouflage.ts`, listed per tank in `appearance.camouflage`), picked on the tank select screen and per unit in the order of battle and shown in both previews. Patterns are procedural in slot space with a per-vehicle seed and cost one extra material class per scheme in use. Paint chips are always darker than the paint they break through, so grey tanks no longer show pale specks.
- Tiger I and Panzer IV wear Zimmerit under their Dunkelgelb schemes (`rendering/zimmerit.ts`): combed columns of horizontal ridges about 2.8 cm apart on upright and sloped armour, as an analytic normal perturbation in the plate's tangent frame with groove occlusion and broken patches. No texture or geometry is added; at 4K on a GTX 1050 Ti the frame time is unchanged.
- `docs/perf/bench.mjs` measures battlefield FPS, p95 frame time, draw calls and triangles across fixed scenarios; `docs/perf/merge-qa.html` diffs merged against authored tanks.

### Layered terrain surfaces
- Grass, dry soil and gravel use local CC0 albedo/normal maps and MIT stochastic hex tiling.
- Signed road distance masks provide smooth diagonal edges independently of terrain vertex spacing; rotated farmland and building yards blend exposed soil.
- The ground is matte, with macro vegetation variation and distance-faded normals.
- Focused terrain mask tests cover diagonal edges, crossings, degenerate roads, rotated farmland and regeneration.
